At Ello, an AI authoring agent called Mesa built the math activities. I wrote the instructions it worked from, checked what it made, and decided what shipped. At Camosun I build AI tutors and feedback tools for my own courses. Each case below is a point where a person had to decide.

## Five cases

**1. The audit agent proposed. I decided.** During a cost-cutting push at Ello, an AI agent reviewed live activities and suggested fixes. I checked each one and confirmed or corrected it in production. My own AI clean-up prompts worked the same way: draft the changes, rank them by severity, apply them only after approval.

**2. The picture that taught the wrong skill.** In two linked activities, some visuals pointed kids toward measurement when the goal was graphing. Fishing hooks fit the pond story and could scare a five-year-old. Expert reviewers caught both. I changed my process: every asset now gets checked against the learning objective, and an expert sees the activity before assets are commissioned. See [Two Math Activities, One Concept](/projects/ello-i-do-we-do/).

**3. The win I did not claim.** Activities I polished finished more often than other math activities (median 49% vs 43%). I reported the number and said the data could not credit the polish. Struggling activities were revised more often, and ratings came only from kids who finished. Either could explain the gap. See [Why Do Kids Quit Math Activities?](/projects/ello-engagement-research/).

**4. The tutor students skipped.** My three AI tutors were carefully designed and linked from the course site. Students used them less than I expected. When stuck, most waited for a person. I published that result, and it shaped my next design: help has to appear at the moment a student gets stuck. See [AI Tutors](/projects/ai-tutors/).

**5. Feedback I still sign.** My lab-report workflow has the AI draft feedback against a rubric and a list of real errors from past semesters. That list keeps it from inventing problems. I read every report next to the draft, fix it, and approve it. See [Smaller Tools](/projects/smaller-tools/#lab-report-feedback).

## Writing for people and for the AI

Most of what I wrote at Ello had two readers.

- **The revision brief** set the rules for questioning a young child, the story arc and the asset briefs. The agent built from it, and the art team worked from the same document.
- **The quality checklist** scored an activity before and after revision. Every rule cites its research, so everyone revising was held to the same bar. See [The Polish Playbook](/projects/ello-polish-playbook/).
- **The tutor prompt** for Charger requires one question per turn, then a pause. The full prompt is on the [AI Tutors](/projects/ai-tutors/) page.

## What I believe about these tools

- AI is quick at a first draft and poor at knowing which change matters. Diagnose first.
- Two big rounds beat six small ones.
- If a rule matters, write it into the prompt.
- Give the model real errors to look for.
- One metric misleads. I score five: start, finish, like, learn, return.
- Ship when it flows, keeps load low and hits its objective. Track the open flags afterwards.
- A good tool behind a link goes unused.
- Hold the tools loosely. They change every few months.
