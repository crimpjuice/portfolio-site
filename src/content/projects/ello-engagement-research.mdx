*Ello · AI Learning Designer, Math (Contractor) · Engagement research and analytics · Oct–Nov 2026*

## The problem

Only 3 of 77 math activities in Ello's app reached the team's 60% completion goal. Polish time was going wherever someone had a hunch, and there was no shared picture of what separated the activities kids finished from the ones they walked away from.

I wanted to know what was actually losing kids, so the next hour of polish went where it would matter.

## What I did

- **Data:** pulled activity data from Mesa, Ello's authoring platform, for 379 activities (about 89,000 attempts) and compared completion, session length, drop-off timing, and ratings across math, reading, and English-language-learning activities.
- **Playtests:** played the high- and low-completion activities myself to see what the numbers couldn't show.
- **Hypothesis test:** I suspected varied interaction and novelty drove engagement, so I checked that against the design tags Mesa stores on each activity.
- **Research:** pulled the learning-science background into a primer the team could use: Mayer's multimedia principles, cognitive load, and seductive details (fun that costs learning).

## What I found

**Kids give a sitting about 5 minutes.** Time per sitting barely changed between activities. Total length did, and each extra minute in math cost about 2.6 points of completion.

**Most quitting happens a few minutes in, not at the start.** Bounces (leaving within 30 seconds) were only 14% of losses. The first round or two after the opening hook mattered most.

**One difficulty step can be a cliff.** In one matched pair, completion fell almost 20 points from Tier 1 to Tier 2 while ratings held steady. Something in the harder tier was stopping kids, not putting them off.

**Variety in how kids interact helps; covering more skills doesn't.** Each extra interaction type went with higher positive ratings. Packing more skills into one activity didn't.

**The app's best finisher teaches almost nothing.** *Would You Rather?* reached about 85% completion by being short, giving a choice every turn, and making kids laugh. That became the benchmark for what engagement feels like. The job is getting a math activity to feel like that while still teaching.

## Rigor

I rated every finding by strength of evidence and named its limits:

- Ratings only come from kids who finished, so they flatter long activities.
- Activities that struggled got revised more often, so "revisions" and "low completion" looked linked for the wrong reason.
- Math activities I'd polished finished more often than the rest (median 49% vs 43%). I said plainly that this couldn't be credited to the polish without a before/after or A/B test.

That last one mattered to me. It would have been easy to claim the win.

## Evidence of impact

> The math team got a ranked polish list, a playtest plan, and four design rules: **keep it short, keep kids busy, offer choices, vary the interactions.** The findings fed directly into [The Polish Playbook](/projects/ello-polish-playbook/), which turned them into how each activity was revised and measured.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

Engagement analytics at scale, survival / drop-off analysis, hypothesis testing against product metadata, mixed-methods research (data, playtests, and literature), and the discipline to separate what the data shows from what it can't yet prove. Also: turning analysis into something a team can act on the next morning.
