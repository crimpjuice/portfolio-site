*Ello · AI Learning Designer, Math (Contractor) · Learning design process · Oct–Nov 2026*

## The problem

Polishing an activity at Ello meant taking a working draft, built with an AI authoring agent, and making it something a five-year-old would actually stick with. In practice it meant many small rounds of tweaks, with no shared standard for "good", no clear way to find the real problem, and no way to tell afterwards whether a change had worked. Lessons from one polish rarely carried over to the next.

## What I built

A playbook that connects my research (The Engagement Primer) and my data analysis ([Why Do Kids Quit Math Activities?](/projects/ello-engagement-research/)) to the day-to-day work of revising an activity.

**A two-hour workflow.** Timed stages: diagnose only → pick a direction (10 minutes, hard stop) → write every change in one pass → run once → correct once → score → ship. Two big rounds beat six small ones.

**Diagnosis before fixing.** Weight the biggest losses first — mishearing, dead air while the tutor responds, first-minute exits — then read which layer is weakest to know where to look.

**A five-layer scorecard.**

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Start | Do kids get past the opening? |
| Finish | Do they reach the end? |
| Like | Do they rate it well? |
| Learn | Does it hit the objective? |
| Return | Do they come back? |

An activity can complete well and still be the lowest rated, so completion alone isn't enough.

**One research-backed quality bar.** A single checklist used both to diagnose and to give the final score: the first 60 seconds, every question, the screen and media, shape and level, and what kids love. Every item cites the research behind it.

**Shared idea banks.** Story worlds, mechanics, and engagement devices, with a record of where each was used, so builders don't repeat themselves.

**AI prompts with a human in the loop.** Reusable prompts gather everything said about an activity and run two clean-up passes. Changes are drafted, ranked by severity, and applied only after approval.

## Measuring results

- Log a baseline before each polish.
- Change one **"test knob"** per polish — an early choice, a song, a length cut — so results can add up across activities.
- Check seven days after shipping, and only judge once there are about 100 attempts on the new version.
- Rule out confounds first: tracking bugs, a shift in the US/Kenya user mix, and ratings that only come from kids who finished.

## Judgement

> **The ship rule:** if the activity flows, keeps load low, hits its objectives, passes the first 60 seconds, and runs 5–10 minutes, ship it with flags open. "Every flag closed" is the wrong bar.

## Evidence of impact

> I used the Playbook to polish 31 activities across 15 K–2 units (time, place value, money, measurement, geometry, comparing numbers, addition and subtraction, area, and data). 25 reached production-ready or went live, including *The Pirate Clock!*, *Submarine Adventure*, and *Mystery at the Smoothie Shop*.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

Process design, quality frameworks grounded in learning science, experimental design for content teams (baselines, single-variable changes, confound checks), and human-in-the-loop AI workflow design. The Playbook is the piece that ties the research, the data, and the daily work into one system.
