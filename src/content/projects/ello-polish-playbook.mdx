*Ello · AI Learning Designer, Math (Contractor) · Oct–Nov 2026*

## The problem

An AI authoring agent built the first draft of each activity. Turning that draft into something a five-year-old would finish took many small rounds of tweaks. There was no shared standard for "good" and no way to tell whether a change worked.

## What I built

**A two-hour workflow.** Diagnose, pick a direction (10 minutes, hard stop), write every change in one pass, run once, correct once, score, ship.

**Diagnosis first.** Start with the biggest losses: mishearing, dead air while the tutor responds, and exits in the first minute.

**A five-part scorecard.**

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Start | Do kids get past the opening? |
| Finish | Do they reach the end? |
| Like | Do they rate it well? |
| Learn | Does it hit the objective? |
| Return | Do they come back? |

An activity can finish well and still be the lowest rated, so one number is never enough.

**One quality checklist.** The same list is used to diagnose and to score. Every item cites its research.

**Shared idea banks** of story worlds, mechanics and engagement devices, so builders don't repeat themselves.

**AI prompts with a person in the loop.** Prompts gather the feedback on an activity and draft fixes ranked by severity. Nothing is applied until someone approves it.

## Measuring results

- Log a baseline before each polish.
- Change one "test knob" per polish, such as an early choice, a song or a shorter length.
- Check after 7 days and about 100 attempts.
- Rule out tracking bugs, shifts in the user mix, and ratings that only come from kids who finished.

**The ship rule:** if it flows, keeps load low, hits its objectives, passes the first 60 seconds and runs 5–10 minutes, ship it and keep the open flags on the list.

## What changed

I used the playbook to polish 31 activities across 15 K–2 units. 25 reached production or went live, including *The Pirate Clock!*, *Submarine Adventure* and *Mystery at the Smoothie Shop*.
