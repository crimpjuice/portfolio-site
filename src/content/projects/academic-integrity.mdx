## The problem

Most responses to cheating focus on detection and policy. I started from a different question: what makes a student decide cheating is worth it?

In my experience, one of three conditions comes first:

1. The work feels unconnected to anything the student cares about.
2. The assessment makes cheating the easy path.
3. The student has stopped believing effort will pay off.

Better detection fixes none of these, so I treated cheating as a design problem.

## Three components (PHYS 104, Camosun)

**1. A day-one survey.** Students tell me their program, why they're taking the course, what they find hard, how they react when stuck and what they hope to get out of it. It shows students I see them as people. The answers also drive personalization: AI rewrites problems and labs around student interests. A kinesiology student gets biomechanics, and a music student gets acoustics. The physics is the same.

**2. The Productive Struggle grade.** 15% of the grade comes from a weekly self-reflection on persistence, group contribution and revising wrong ideas. Later weeks count more, and the lowest week is dropped. Honest effort earns marks even when the answer is wrong, and copying earns nothing. The full rubric is on [How I Teach](/how-i-teach/#the-productive-struggle-grade).

**3. An AI tutor with guardrails.** The tutor never gives answers. It asks the next question and waits for the student's reasoning, so there is nothing to copy. Repeat support requests fell **22% in one semester**.

> AI didn't create cheating. It made the exit door bigger. Good design makes that exit less appealing at every layer.

## The minimum version

Any instructor can do this without AI:

1. **Ask early why students are here,** and learn something that changes how you see them.
2. **Grade effort and process** in at least one assessment.
3. **Show students their answers changed something.** If the survey disappears into silence, trust erodes.

The same moves that reduce cheating also raise engagement, attendance and retention.
