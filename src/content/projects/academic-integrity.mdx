## The problem

Academic integrity is almost always treated as a compliance problem. Institutions write policies, design detection systems, and redesign assessments to close loopholes. These are real responses to a real problem. They address the symptom, not the cause.

The more useful question is: what makes a student decide cheating is worth it?

After years in the classroom, at Camosun College, Algonquin College, and in over 100 one-on-one tutoring relationships, my answer is that cheating is a rational response to a meaning failure. It happens when the cost-benefit of genuine effort collapses. And that collapse is almost always preceded by one of three conditions:

1. **The student doesn't see the connection between what they're learning and what they care about.** The work feels arbitrary. The grade is the only reason to do it — and if the grade can be obtained another way, why not?
2. **The assessment design makes cheating the path of least resistance.** A take-home exam, an easily searchable question, a final paper on a generic topic — these are structures that don't make cheating feel pointless.
3. **The student has lost hope that effort will pay off.** When students don't believe the credential means anything, or don't believe they're capable of earning it honestly, the calculation changes entirely.

Notice that none of these three conditions is solved by better detection.

## The framework

The intervention is two-directional: increase intrinsic motivation, and shrink the pathways to cheating. AI can serve both, but only if the educator is asking the right questions first.

The questions start here:

- Why is this student here?
- What are they hoping this course does for them?
- Does this assessment make genuine engagement feel worthwhile?
- Does this student believe their effort has a future payoff?

These questions assume students know their own answers — and many don't. So the deeper question becomes: *can we bring into light, for the educator and the student together, the core motivation and intention behind being in this course?*

Once those questions are sufficiently answered, course policy and assessment design become much easier to build. The work isn't enforcement. It's design.

## The system in practice

This framework is implemented across three interconnected components in PHYS 104 at Camosun College.

### Component 1 — The day-one survey: "Tell me who you are"

A custom-built HTML intake survey given on the first day of class. Students share their program, their reason for taking the course, their prior background, what they do outside school, what they've found confusing or frustrating in math and physics before, how they respond when stuck, what they hope the course does for them, and something about themselves that wouldn't show up on a transcript.

This is not an administrative intake form. It's the first signal to a student that this instructor sees them as a whole person — not a grade, not a seat. That signal matters particularly for first-generation students, students returning after time away, and students with anxiety or learning disabilities who may have already decided they don't belong in a STEM classroom.

Critically, the survey data feeds directly into course personalization. Using AI, problem sets and lab experiments are recontextualized to match student interests and goals identified in the survey. A student interested in exercise science gets biomechanics contexts. A student interested in music gets acoustics. The physics is identical. Relevance is not a motivational trick. It's a cognitive one. Meaningful context reduces extraneous load and increases genuine engagement.

### Component 2 — The Productive Struggle Score

A 15% grade component assessed weekly through a short self-reflection submitted on D2L. Students score themselves on persistence, contribution to group thinking, revision of understanding, and willingness to attempt hard problems without knowing where to start.

The grade is weighted toward later weeks — growth matters more than starting position. The lowest week is dropped. Instructor calibration is light: the default is to trust the student's self-assessment.

What this component does to academic integrity: it makes effort explicitly and structurally valuable. A student who is trying honestly — even failing — is earning real marks. A student who coasts and copies earns nothing here regardless of their final exam score. It also builds the metacognitive habit of asking "how am I actually engaging with this?" weekly. That habit is the opposite of the dissociation that enables cheating.

The full rubric and self-assessment instrument are documented on [the Productive Struggle Score page](/productive-struggle/).

### Component 3 — The AI tutor with guardrails

A course-specific AI tutoring assistant engineered to build self-sufficiency rather than dependency. The core design constraint: the tutor never gives answers. It asks the right question at the right moment, prompting the student to articulate their reasoning before receiving any feedback.

This directly counters the most common form of AI-enabled academic dishonesty: the student who asks an AI for an answer and copies it. A tutor that requires the student to reason first cannot be gamed the same way.

Measured outcome: **22% reduction in repeat support requests within one semester**, indicating students were building genuine problem-solving independence rather than returning with the same questions.

## The through-line

> Students cheat when effort feels disconnected from meaning. AI doesn't create that problem, it just makes the exit door bigger. The solution is better design: of assessments, of feedback loops, and of the relationship between what students are learning and why it matters to them.

When a student feels seen on day one, when their problem sets connect to their actual interests, when effort is explicitly graded and growth-weighted, and when the AI tutor coaches them toward understanding rather than handing them answers, cheating becomes structurally less appealing at every layer. By design, not by rule.

## What this means for institutions

The same interventions that reduce academic dishonesty also improve engagement, attendance, and retention. Students who feel connected to their reasons for being in a course don't just cheat less. They stay enrolled.

The minimum viable version of this framework — the elements that transfer across disciplines without requiring technical skill — comes down to three non-negotiables:

1. **A genuine intake moment.** Something structured and early that asks students why they're here. The bar: does the instructor learn something that changes how they see at least some students?
2. **At least one assessment that grades effort and process, not just correctness.** The signal: your engagement has value independent of whether you got it right.
3. **Closing the loop visibly.** Students need to see that the intake data did something. If the survey disappears into silence, trust erodes.

None of these require AI. AI is what makes the instructor's version scalable and rich. But the underlying moves are human and low-barrier — which means they generalize across disciplines.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

This project demonstrates systems-level instructional design: three components built as a single coherent intervention, grounded in motivation science (self-determination theory, intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation) and connected explicitly to institutional retention outcomes. It also demonstrates AI prompt engineering for constrained tutoring, formative assessment design, and the kind of intake-to-personalization workflow that scales across disciplines and contexts.
