## The problem

Most courses begin with a roster and end with a grade. What happens in between is largely designed for an imagined average student who doesn't exist in any actual room. Instructors who want to personalize have traditionally faced a hard constraint: gathering meaningful learner data takes time, and acting on it at scale takes more time than most teaching loads allow.

AI changes that constraint. It doesn't eliminate the need for human judgment about what to do with learner data. It eliminates the time cost of acting on it.

## The system

This is a four-layer system, each layer feeding the next.

### Layer 1: Day-one intake survey

A custom-built survey administered on the first day of class. Students share their program, their reason for taking the course, their prior background in the subject, what they do outside school, what they've found frustrating in similar courses before, how they respond when stuck, what they hope the course does for them, and something about themselves that wouldn't show up on a transcript.

The survey is not an administrative form. It's the first signal to a learner that this instructor sees them as a whole person. That signal matters particularly for first-generation students, students returning after time away, and students with anxiety or prior negative experience in STEM who may have already decided they don't belong.

### Layer 2: AI-assisted grouping

Survey responses are processed with AI assistance to identify clusters of shared interests, working styles, and strengths. Groups are formed around these clusters rather than randomly or alphabetically.

The rationale: groups built around common curiosity sustain more genuine collaborative conversation. A student who loves music and a student who loves sports may both be learning wave physics, but they will have richer discussions if the problems they're working through connect to a context they both find interesting.

This also gives the instructor a map of the room before the first group activity begins.

### Layer 3: Personalized problem sets, labs & talking points

Survey data feeds directly into course content. Using AI, problem sets, lab experiments, in-class examples, and discussion prompts are recontextualized to match student interests identified in the intake survey.

The physics (or mathematics, or any subject) is identical across all variants. The context changes. A student interested in exercise science gets biomechanics. A student interested in music gets acoustics. A student interested in architecture gets structural loads.

This is not a motivational trick. Meaningful context reduces extraneous cognitive load and increases genuine engagement — a well-documented effect in cognitive load theory. The content becomes easier to process because the brain isn't simultaneously learning the physics and learning to care about an unfamiliar scenario.

### Layer 4: Weekly check-in surveys

Short surveys sent weekly throughout the term. Students report on their current level of understanding, what's connecting and what isn't, their motivation this week, and anything they want the instructor to know.

Responses are reviewed before each class and used to adjust that session in real time: which concepts need more time, which students need a direct check-in, where the energy in the room is and why.

This creates a continuous feedback loop between learner experience and instructional decision-making, with a much shorter lag than waiting for midterm results or office hours.

## What this system does to the instructor's role

The instructor who runs this system spends less time guessing and more time responding. The first class is not a generic introduction to a generic group of students. It's the second data point in a conversation that started before anyone walked in the door.

More substantively: this system operationalizes the belief that learner curiosity is not a given. It has to be found, named, and connected to the material. The intake survey is the instrument for finding it. The personalization layer is the instrument for connecting it. The weekly surveys are the instrument for maintaining it.

> Impact evidence — survey response rates, grouping outcomes, qualitative feedback on personalized problem sets — is being collected across the current term and will be reported here as it consolidates.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

This project demonstrates learner needs analysis and audience research at intake, data-informed instructional decision-making throughout a learning experience, and AI-assisted personalization at scale. The system architecture — structured intake, cluster analysis, content variant generation, continuous feedback loop — transfers directly to corporate L&D contexts: onboarding programs, training needs assessments, employee development tracks, and any learning experience where knowing who the learner is before designing for them would improve the outcome.
