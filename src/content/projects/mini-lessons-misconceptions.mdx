> **Deployment:** These lessons were built as standalone HTML files and embedded directly into D2L/Brightspace via SCORM packaging. Students accessed them within the LMS — no external link, no separate login. Completion tracking and quiz response data were captured through D2L's engagement analytics, enabling the iteration process described below.

## The design problem

Some student errors aren't random. They're systematic. The same wrong answers appear semester after semester because students hold coherent but incorrect mental models of how the physical world works. Telling students the correct answer doesn't fix this. Research consistently shows that misconceptions must be surfaced and confronted — made visible to the student — before replacement learning can occur.

Misconception-tackling mini-lessons are built around this research finding.

## Design principles

### Activate the misconception first

The lesson begins by presenting a scenario specifically designed to elicit the misconception — to get students to commit to the wrong answer before any correction occurs. This is counterintuitive but essential: a student who hasn't stated the misconception hasn't confronted it. Prediction-first design forces the commitment.

### Create productive cognitive conflict

After the student commits to their prediction, the lesson reveals what actually happens — and makes the conflict impossible to ignore. This is the moment of cognitive dissonance that makes learning possible. The design must make the gap between prediction and reality vivid and undeniable, not easy to rationalize away.

### Provide the correct model explicitly

After conflict, students need a clear, well-structured replacement model — not just "you were wrong." The correct explanation is presented simply, with the specific feature of the situation that the misconception caused them to misinterpret highlighted directly.

### Verify with a transfer problem

The lesson closes with a transfer problem in a different surface context — same underlying concept, different scenario. This tests whether the conceptual replacement has occurred or whether the student has just learned the specific case.

## How misconceptions were identified

Target misconceptions were drawn from prior semester exam item analysis, physics education research literature, and informal classroom observation of recurring errors. Each lesson targets one specific, well-documented misconception.

## Evidence of impact

> Students who completed misconception-tackling lessons showed measurably better performance on related exam items compared to prior cohorts. Post-lesson reflection prompts revealed that students could articulate *why* their original thinking was incorrect — a stronger indicator of conceptual change than correct answers alone.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

This project demonstrates conceptual change instructional design grounded in physics education research, formative assessment used to identify and target specific error patterns, and interactive HTML content development with SCORM/LMS integration. The iteration process — moving from exam item analysis to targeted lesson design to re-measurement — is the core transferable skill and applies across any domain where persistent learner misconceptions need to be addressed systematically.
