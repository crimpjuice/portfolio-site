Two workshops for working instructors. Both were judged the same way, by what people did in their own courses afterwards.

## The problem

Instructors are busy experts. They will sit through a session about a new tool, agree with it, and change nothing. Generic AI workshops ("here's ChatGPT, try it!") have that problem. What colleagues needed both times was a process they could own and repeat without me.

## Workshop 1: moving online, Algonquin College (2020)

When courses moved online in 2020, I ran a peer workshop for 5 STEM instructors on multimedia learning and online course design, built on Mayer's multimedia principles and adult learning theory.

- I started with an informal needs analysis to find out what was actually stopping people from using the LMS well.
- Each instructor left with one evidence-based change to make in a course they were teaching that term.
- I measured confidence before and after, then checked back to see what had changed.

> Confidence rose by an average of 4 points on a 10-point scale. **4 of the 5 instructors made an evidence-based change to their course within 10 days.**

## Workshop 2: building with AI, Camosun College

Faculty at Camosun knew AI tools existed and were curious, but had no concrete workflow they trusted.

### Teaching the workflow, not the tool

The workshop follows the design process. Find the concept students get stuck on. Structure a mini-lesson around it. Prompt the AI to draft content inside your constraints. Then evaluate what it gives you and fix it. The AI tool is one step in that process.

### Peer credibility

I used my own mini-lessons as the live examples, including the before-and-after, the prompts I actually used, and the revisions I made. A colleague showing real work is easier to trust than a vendor demo.

### Building during the session

Participants built a mini-lesson draft in the room. Everyone left with a working prototype to take back to a course and refine.

> **Two instructors went on to build interactive, AI-enhanced content for their own courses without me.** A department-wide version is in development.

## What I learned

Faculty don't resist AI because they dislike technology. They resist because nobody has answered "what do I actually do with this on Monday?" A workflow answers that. The same was true of online teaching in 2020.

## Transferable skills demonstrated

- Designing training for experienced adults that changes what they do at work
- Needs analysis before building, and follow-up measurement after
- Coaching peers through a change they did not ask for
- Teaching a repeatable AI workflow rather than a tool
