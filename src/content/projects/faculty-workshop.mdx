## The problem

Faculty at Camosun were aware that AI tools existed and curious about applying them to teaching, but lacked a concrete, subject-agnostic workflow they could follow confidently. Generic AI workshops ("here's ChatGPT, try it!") don't produce lasting instructional change. What colleagues needed was a structured process they could own and repeat independently.

## Design decisions

### Teaching the workflow, not the tool

The workshop was designed around the *design process* — how to identify a conceptual friction point, how to structure a mini-lesson around it, how to prompt AI to generate content within instructional constraints, and how to evaluate and iterate the result. The AI tool is a step in the process, not the point of it.

### Peer credibility as a design asset

Being a faculty member running a workshop for faculty members matters. I drew on my own mini-lesson work as live examples — showing the before-and-after, the prompts I actually used, the iterations I made. This is more credible than a vendor demo and more actionable than an abstract training.

### Hands-on construction during the session

Participants didn't just watch — they built a mini-lesson draft in real time during the workshop. By the end, every participant had a working prototype they could take back to their course and refine. This dramatically increases the likelihood of actual adoption.

## Evidence of impact

> Faculty participants successfully built their own interactive mini-lessons independently following the workshop, applying the workflow to pedagogical challenges identified in their own courses.

## Reflection

The biggest insight from running this workshop: faculty don't resist AI because they're technophobic. They resist it because they don't have a clear answer to "what do I actually do with this on Monday?" A workflow gives them that answer.
