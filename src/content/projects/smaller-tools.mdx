## Lab report feedback

**The problem:** a section of 30 lab reports takes hours to mark, and much of the feedback repeats.

**The workflow:**

1. Give the AI the rubric and a list of common errors from past semesters.
2. The AI scores each criterion and drafts feedback that cites the rubric.
3. I read each report next to the draft, correct it and approve it.

The error list keeps the AI from inventing problems. The prompt also sets the audience, the tone and a short length, so students actually read the feedback.

**The result:** feedback cycles take much less time, and the time saved goes to students.

<details>
<summary>View the working prompt (buoyancy lab example)</summary>

```text
I am a physics instructor and I will be correcting several lab reports on
buoyancy and Archimedes principle. I was hoping you could be my assistant
throughout this task. I will double check your work. I will give you lab
reports, Evaluation Rubric, Report Expectations and the experiment
procedure. You will leave concise feedback in simple and plain language
while grading elements of the report deducting only whole point values
when needed. You will output the final feedback as bulleted list of
comments referencing the rubric section. You will also leave a final tally
of the score. Keep the language neutral to somewhat positive. Common
places where students lose marks are:

- Not obeying significant figures and precision in gathering their
  measurements and final calculations. (Remember: every measurement needs
  uncertainty to one sigfig, and the measurement precision must match the
  sigfig precision.)
- Not doing a consistency check to compare experiment and theoretical values
- Not writing procedure in past tense AND bullet points
- Not being careful about professional formatting
- Not describing and introducing tables and graphs
- In discussion: not identifying VALID sources of error and not digging
  deep enough into the connection between the sources of error, the
  theory, the procedure they followed, the final results and their
  intertwining effects on each other
- In discussion: not providing sufficient recommendations to changes in
  the procedure that address the sources of error
- In conclusion: not recapping the experiment and results with error

When you're ready I will give you the first report. Do not include emojis.

Here are the Report Requirements:
[INSERT REPORT REQUIREMENTS & ELEMENTS]
```

</details>

## Problem extraction and difficulty sorting

**The problem:** open textbooks have good problems, but their notation and terms differ from my course's.

**The workflow:** one prompt pulls the problems from a chapter and rewrites them in course notation and terms. It flags any problem where a rewrite might change the meaning. A second prompt sorts the problems into Foundational, Intermediate and Advanced, with a short reason for each. The output is LaTeX.

**The result:** a course-aligned problem bank, sorted for scaffolded assignments, without hours of manual work.

<details>
<summary>View the extraction prompt (DC Circuits example)</summary>

```text
I need assistance making a problem set for my physics course (I am the
instructor). I've attached a screenshot of the module titles for
reference - Module 9 is on DC Circuits and I want to build a problem set
for this module. I've also attached six files from which you will draw
the problems from (these problems are from an open source physics
textbook). I want you to copy WORD FOR WORD the ODD numbered problems
only and organize them by the Physics 295 Module titles. Now, in the
course, we actually skipped 9.5: "Parallel and Series Circuits", so you
may simply write "Section skipped".

Some of these problems have images - when you encounter an image do not
worry - I will manually place the image in the final document. Your task
will be to simply indicate that an image is missing with "IMAGE GOES HERE".

In addition, in the attached files are the answers to the odd numbered
questions. So it will also be your task to pair up the questions you copy
to the corresponding answer.

Now, the final output should be formatted as the first section
"EXERCISES" followed by a section "ANSWERS". The crucial piece is that
the Exercises are renumbered starting from 1 counting up by one, instead
of counting up by odd numbers. The Answer key must follow.

Lastly, output everything as LATEX so I can bring it over to Overleaf.
```

</details>

<details>
<summary>View the classification prompt</summary>

```text
Identify, organize, and label the attached physics problems by
difficulty. There are three difficulty levels. Hard problems can be
identified when they have about 70% of the following attributes: they
rely on concepts to solve, math heavy, have data interpretation, real
world application, hidden information and assumptions, integrate physics
topics.

Easy problems are problems that probe concepts or terminology or

Output the newly organized problems as Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3

solutions.

Output as LaTeX so it can be brought over to Overleaf.
```

## Learner intake and grouping

**The problem:** most courses are built for an average student who isn't actually in the room.

**The system:**

1. A day-one survey on each student's program, goals, interests and what they do when stuck.
2. AI-assisted grouping around shared interests.
3. Problem sets and labs rewritten around those interests, with the same physics.
4. Short weekly check-ins that shape the next class.

**The result:** impact data is being collected this term.

## Gamified problem sets

**The problem:** traditional homework gives late feedback and no sense of progress, so students avoid it.

**The design:** instant feedback with a short explanation, a visible progress bar, and problems that start easy and get harder. AI helped generate problems matched to course topics and student interests.

**The result:** students said they were more willing to attempt problems on their own, and voluntary practice was higher than with traditional sets in earlier semesters.
