When someone asks me what I teach, the honest answer is: I teach people how to think through hard problems. Physics is the medium. The real work is building the cognitive habits — careful reasoning, structured problem decomposition, willingness to sit with confusion — that transfer far beyond any particular subject.

That answer used to feel complete. Over the last year, AI has changed what's possible in my classroom, not by doing the teaching, but by clearing the space for a different kind of teaching. The administrative load, the one-size-fits-all problem sets, the lag between spotting a misconception and being able to respond to it: AI has reduced all of that. What that reduction has made room for is the question I now spend most of my time on: what kind of experience should a learner have in this room?

My approach to instruction is grounded in learning science, not intuition. Five principles guide everything I design. A sixth, newer one has emerged from the classroom redesign I've been building this year.

## Design for Cognitive Load, Not Content Coverage

My Concordia thesis grounded me in Sweller's cognitive load theory — the insight that working memory is severely limited, and that instruction must actively manage what competes for that limited resource. This means I sequence content carefully, strip extraneous elements that don't serve the learning objective, and favour worked examples with self-explanation prompts over passive content delivery.

In practice: before I build anything, I ask what the learner needs to hold in mind simultaneously, and whether the design makes that easier or harder.

## Measure What Matters, Then Iterate

Every instructional intervention I make has a feedback loop. At Brilliant.org it was engagement metrics and content drop-off analytics. At Camosun it's D2L engagement data, support request patterns, and qualitative learner feedback. I treat instructional design as a continuous product cycle, not a one-time delivery.

The 22% reduction in repeat support requests from my AI physics tutor didn't happen by accident. It came from watching what wasn't working, forming a hypothesis, adjusting the design, and tracking what changed.

## AI as a Design Material, Held Lightly

I approach AI tools the way a thoughtful designer approaches any medium: by asking what affordances it offers and what constraints it imposes. A well-designed AI tutor can provide patient, infinitely scalable practice. It cannot replicate the sense-making that happens in genuine dialogue with an expert. The design challenge is knowing precisely where each belongs.

This is why I engineer guardrails into every AI tool I build for learners. A tutor that gives answers teaches dependence. A tutor that asks the right question at the right moment teaches reasoning.

But there's a second, equally important design principle here: non-attachment. AI tools and platforms change fast enough that anchoring a pedagogy to any specific one is a category error. What I hold onto are the underlying design decisions: what problem am I solving, what does this tool make possible that I couldn't do before, and what human judgment does it still require? Those questions travel. The tools themselves won't.

## Confidence Is a Prerequisite, Not a Reward

In STEM education, the single biggest barrier to learning is often not a knowledge gap; it's a confidence gap. Students who believe they cannot do mathematics will not try, and students who don't try will not learn. My tutoring tools are designed confidence-first: low-stakes, patient, and structured to give students early wins before raising difficulty.

## The Best Instruction Is Invisible

When a lesson is well-designed, learners don't notice the scaffolding — they just feel themselves understanding. That's the standard I hold my work to. From the pacing of an interactive mini-lesson to the tone of an AI coach's response, every design decision should serve the learner's experience, not announce itself.

## Design for the Experiences Only Humans Can Have

AI handles certain things well: patient, scalable practice; immediate feedback; infinite customization; tireless availability. These are genuine affordances, and I use them. But they describe a narrow band of what learning actually is.

Deep learning — the kind that changes how someone sees the world — happens through experiences that are irreducibly human. Being genuinely surprised by a physical phenomenon. Working through something hard alongside other people. Caring about a question because it's beautiful or strange or personally meaningful. Feeling the satisfaction of understanding something that was previously opaque. Finding pride in something you're getting good at. The particular grit that comes from sitting with difficulty long enough to break through it.

The research behind each of these is substantial. Gruber, Gelman, and Ranganath (2014, *Neuron*) showed using fMRI that curiosity activates the brain's dopaminergic reward circuit and hippocampal memory systems, improving retention not just for the topic of interest but for incidental material encountered in the same state. Curiosity, in other words, primes the brain for learning at a neurological level. Ryan and Deci's Self-Determination Theory, supported by a meta-analysis of over 200,000 students across 344 samples (Howard et al., 2021), shows that intrinsic motivation and perceived relevance to personal goals are among the strongest predictors of persistence and academic success across educational levels and cultural contexts. Hidi and Renninger's four-phase model of interest development demonstrates that situational interest — the kind triggered by a well-designed classroom moment — can develop into durable individual interest over time. Interest, like curiosity, is not a fixed trait. It can be cultivated. Vygotsky's foundational work on the zone of proximal development, and the contemporary research it has generated, consistently shows that collaborative struggle with peers produces qualitatively richer learning than solo work, and that motivation itself is socially negotiated. And Bjork and Bjork's desirable difficulties framework (1994, extended by Kapur's productive failure research, 2008) demonstrates that struggle and difficulty, when well-designed, produce stronger long-term retention and transfer than instruction that minimizes friction.

None of this is new. What is new is that AI has removed the logistical constraints that previously made designing for these conditions impractical at scale. Personalizing content to individual interests used to mean hours of extra work per student. Responding to a misconception the same day it appeared used to be impossible. Running a learning experience that simultaneously tracks attendance, surveys motivation, and generates targeted follow-up required staffing no single instructor had. AI changes that arithmetic. The research has always known what works. The constraint was always the instructor's time and cognitive bandwidth.

Researchers studying attention and focus have also documented a genuine crisis in learners' ability to sustain concentration, driven by a decade of smartphones and social platforms designed to fragment it (Haidt, 2024; Mark, 2023). At the same time, studies on peer-versus-AI interaction in learning contexts find that students treat AI as a transactional information source even when designed for collaboration, while human peers generate qualitatively richer sense-making conversations. My students' own survey responses reflect this: some welcome AI tools, others distrust the accuracy of outputs, worry about data security, or simply prefer working through hard problems with a person whose judgment they trust. That preference isn't resistance to technology. It's a reasonable response to something real, grounded in what social constructivism has always said about knowledge: that it's built in relationship, through dialogue with trusted others.

These aren't soft extras layered onto real instruction. They are the mechanism. And they've been systematically crowded out of the traditional classroom, not because educators didn't value them, but because the logistics of scale made them hard to sustain.

AI changes that calculation. When it handles what it's good at, the classroom can be reclaimed for what only happens in a room with humans in it: curiosity, awe, collaboration, grit, the slow burn of becoming genuinely good at something.
