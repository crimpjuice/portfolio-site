I design learning experiences that change how people think and what they can do. The thread across ten years of instruction, eight years building content at [Brilliant.org](https://brilliant.org), and two years redesigning a physics course from the ground up is always the same: start with a human problem, build something evidence-informed, measure what changes, and iterate.

My approach to AI follows the same logic. I use it to handle what doesn't require human judgment — drafting, scaffolding, generating, automating — so that what's actually scarce (time, attention, presence in the room) goes toward work only a human can do. That also means taking seriously what my students have told me: that they're genuinely divided about AI, that some distrust its outputs, and that many still prefer working through hard problems with a person they trust. Those responses are design inputs, not obstacles. And it means holding AI tools loosely. They change too rapidly to anchor a philosophy around any specific one. The underlying design principles are what travel.

I've spent the last year rebuilding a physics course from first principles around the conditions that produce genuine, lasting learning. The results — better questions, stronger attendance, more sophisticated student errors — are documented in [The Human Classroom](/human-classroom/).

## Who I am

I'm a physics instructor at Camosun College with a graduate background in learning science — specifically how people build understanding of mathematical and scientific concepts under real cognitive constraints. My graduate thesis examined cognitive load theory and multimedia learning in probability education, and that research lens shapes every design decision I make.

I'm not simply adopting AI tools in the classroom. I'm designing *systems*. Each initiative starts with a clearly defined learning problem, uses evidence to frame a solution, and gets measured and iterated. That's instructional design thinking, not tech enthusiasm. It also means staying genuinely non-attached to any specific tool. AI changes fast enough that the designers who anchor on principles rather than platforms will outlast those who don't.

I spent eight years as a content editor and learning designer at [Brilliant.org](https://brilliant.org) — building interactive lessons in mathematics, physics, and computer science for a global digital platform, collaborating with Product and Engineering teams, and using engagement analytics to drive content decisions. That experience gave me the cross-functional language that bridges pedagogy and product.
