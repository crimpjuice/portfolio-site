Ampère's Law
MODULE 1 / 7

The dot product: how much of B goes along the path

?

A magnetic field can be enormous and still contribute nothing along a path. How?

B∥ = the part of B that points along the step

Everything in Ampère's law is built out of one small product. Take a tiny step dℓ along some path. Now ask a simple question: how much of the field B points the same way as your step? That part is B∥ = |B| cos θ. Multiply it by the length of the step and you have B · dℓ.

With your numbers
Think about it

At what angle is B · dℓ biggest? When is it zero? When does it go negative, and what does a negative value mean?

Show answer
Biggest at θ = 0°, when B points exactly along the step. Zero at θ = 90°: B points sideways and none of it goes along the step. Past 90°, B∥ points backward, against the step, so the product is negative. The sign tells you whether you are walking with the field or against it.
Key insight

Only B∥, the part of B along the step, counts. A field perpendicular to the path is invisible to B · dℓ, no matter how strong it is.

MODULE 2 / 7

From little steps to an integral

?

How do you add up a field along a curve when both the field and the direction of the curve keep changing?

Chop, multiply, add, then shrink the steps

A real path curves, and a real field changes from place to place. So we cheat a little. Chop the path into N straight steps Δℓ. On each step, pretend B is constant (use its value at the start of the step), compute B · Δℓ, and add them all up.

With a few big steps the answer is off. Make the steps smaller and the cheat gets better and better. The number the sum settles on is the line integral.

With your numbers
Think about it

With N = 2 the sum misses badly. What exactly goes wrong with big steps?

Show answer
Two things. The straight step cuts across the curve, so its direction is wrong for most of the stretch. And B changes along the step, but we only used its value at the start. Both errors shrink as the steps shrink, so the sum closes in on one number: the integral.
Key insight

∫ B · dℓ means: chop the path into steps so small that B and the direction are constant over each one, then add up B · dℓ for every step.

MODULE 3 / 7

When B runs along the path

?

Is there ever a way to do a line integral with no calculus at all?

Condition 1: B ∥ dℓ everywhereCondition 2: |B| constant along the path

Suppose B points along the path at every point and has the same size everywhere. Then θ = 0 for every step, cos θ = 1, and every step contributes its full |B| dℓ. Adding them up is easy: |B| times the total length L. Bend the path more and it gets longer, so the integral grows.

With your numbers (|B| = 1.5)
Think about it

Here bending the path always increases the integral. Would that still be true if B pointed in one fixed direction instead of following the path?

Show answer
No. With a fixed B, bending the path makes it longer, but it also tilts the steps away from B, which lowers cos θ. The two effects compete. Here B follows the path, so cos θ stays at 1 and every extra bit of length counts in full. You will see the fixed-B case next.
Key insight

Two conditions make the integral easy: B ∥ dℓ everywhere, and |B| constant along the path. Then ∫ B · dℓ = |B| L. Hold on to these two conditions. They are the whole trick to using Ampère's law.

MODULE 4 / 7

A uniform field: only the endpoints matter

?

Wiggle a path as wildly as you like between the same two points. Does ∫ B · dℓ change?

True when B is uniform (same size and direction everywhere)

Now the opposite situation: B is the same everywhere, and the path wanders around. Green steps go with the field, red steps go against it. Because B is the same everywhere, you can pull it out of the integral. What's left, ∫ dℓ, is all the little steps added head to tail: the displacement Δr from the start A to where you are now, P. So the wiggles drop out completely.

With your numbers (|B| = 1.5)
Same number every time, no matter how much you wiggle.
Think about it

Join the two ends to make a closed loop in this uniform field. What is ∮ B · dℓ?

Show answer
Zero. A closed loop ends where it starts, so Δr = 0. Remember this: a uniform field like this one has no current running through your loop. In two modules you will see that this is no coincidence.
Key insight

In a uniform field, ∫ B · dℓ = B · Δr. Only the endpoints matter, and around any closed loop the answer is zero. The ∮ symbol just means "integral around a closed loop".

MODULE 5 / 7

The field around a long straight wire

?

A current makes a field that circles around the wire. What path would line up perfectly with it?

Direction: circles around the wire (right-hand rule)μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A

Now real physics. A long straight wire carries a current I out of the page (the dot in the middle). The field it makes runs in circles around the wire. Point your right thumb along the current and your fingers curl the way B goes: counterclockwise here. The strength falls off with distance as 1/r.

We take this formula as given for now. It comes from experiment, and later in the course you can derive it from the Biot–Savart law.

With your numbers
Think about it

Take the dashed circle as your path. What is the angle between B and dℓ at each point? Is |B| the same all the way around?

Show answer
θ = 0 everywhere: B is tangent to the circle, exactly along dℓ. And every point on the circle is the same distance r from the wire, so |B| is the same all the way around. Both conditions from Module 3 are met.
Key insight

Around a long straight wire, a circle centred on the wire is the perfect path: B ∥ dℓ everywhere and |B| constant. That makes the integral easy.

MODULE 6 / 7

Closing the loop: the radius cancels

?

Move the loop farther from the wire and the field gets weaker. Does the integral get smaller?

Weaker field × longer loop = same answer

Walk all the way around the circle. Both conditions from Module 3 hold, so the integral is |B| times the length of the path, 2πr. Now watch what happens to r.

With your numbers
Think about it

Make the loop bigger. |B| gets weaker. Why doesn't the integral shrink?

Show answer
|B| falls like 1/r, but the length of the loop grows like r. Double r: the field is half as strong, but the loop is twice as long. The two effects cancel exactly, so the integral depends only on I.
Key insight

For any circle around the wire, ∮ B · dℓ = μ₀I. The size of the loop drops out. The only thing left is the current.

MODULE 7 / 7

Any loop at all: the full statement

?

Squash the circle into a blob, or slide it off the wire entirely. What survives?

Only the "around the wire" part of each step counts

Is a circle special? Split any step dℓ into two pieces: a piece pointing straight away from the wire, and a piece going around it, of length r dφ (dφ is the small angle the step sweeps out, seen from the wire). B circles the wire, so it is perpendicular to the "away" piece, which contributes nothing. Only the "around" piece counts, and for that piece the r cancels again, step by step.

So each step contributes μ₀I/2π times the angle it sweeps. The integral just counts angle. Follow the violet line from the wire to your position as you trace the loop.

With your numbers (I = 10 A)
Think about it

Slide the loop until the wire is outside it. Some steps are green, some are red. Why do they cancel exactly?

Show answer
Watch the violet line from the wire to your position. If the wire is outside, the line swings one way, then swings back, and ends where it started. Net angle: 0, so the integral is 0. If the wire is inside, the line makes one full turn, 2π, so the integral is μ₀I. Only current that the loop goes around counts.
Key insight

∮ B · dℓ depends only on the current the loop encloses. Not the shape, not the size, not the position. That is Ampère's law.

⚡ How to use Ampère's law in practice

Ampère's law is always true. It is only useful for finding B when you can pick a loop that makes the left side easy, which means bringing back the two conditions from Module 3.

1
Use symmetry to figure out which way B points and what it can depend on.
2
Choose a loop made of pieces where B ∥ dℓ with |B| constant, or B ⊥ dℓ (or B = 0) so the piece contributes nothing.
3
Left side: ∮ B · dℓ = |B| L, where L is the length of the parallel pieces. Right side: μ₀ Ienc. Solve for |B|.
